Keep the music, protect the hearing

Safe-listening tools and habits that preserve the joy and detail of music without treating hearing as an unlimited resource.

Published 2026-09-06Updated 2026-09-06
  • Music
  • Hearing
  • Digital wellbeing

The invisible dose

Risk depends on loudness and time together.

A sound does not need to hurt to contribute to hearing damage. The relevant exposure is a combination of level, duration, and frequency. The World Health Organization gives 80 decibels for up to 40 hours per week as an adult safe-listening reference; at 90 decibels, the corresponding time falls to four hours per week.

That trade-off matters more than a single volume-slider position. Different headphones produce different sound levels at the same percentage, recordings vary, and a listener often raises the volume to overcome buses, machinery, crowds, or aircraft. A device that reports sound exposure is more informative than one that shows only “70 percent.”

Use the tools

Make the device remember what ears cannot measure.

Look for a phone or music player that tracks both level and duration, summarizes a weekly sound allowance, warns before exposure becomes excessive, and offers a volume limit. The WHO and International Telecommunication Union recommend these capabilities in their safe-listening device standard.

Use well-fitted headphones, and consider active noise cancellation in steady background noise so the music does not have to compete. Noise cancellation does not make any chosen volume safe by itself; it simply removes one reason to turn the volume up. Keep exposure alerts enabled instead of dismissing them as interruptions.

  • Enable headphone-level and exposure notifications where supported.
  • Set a conservative volume limit before a long journey or work session.
  • Check the weekly exposure summary, not only today’s peak.
  • Lower the source of background noise or move away from it when possible.
  • Take quiet breaks during long sessions.

Live music

Protect the ear without leaving the experience.

At concerts, rehearsals, clubs, festivals, and sporting events, move away from loudspeakers and use well-fitted hearing protection. Music-oriented earplugs aim to reduce the level more evenly across frequencies than basic foam plugs, although fit and verified attenuation still matter. Carry them before the event begins; protection left at home has no effect.

Venues also have responsibility. WHO’s global standard recommends sound-level monitoring, audience information, hearing protection, quiet zones, and staff training alongside an upper average sound-level limit. Safe listening should be part of good production, not a burden placed only on the audience.

Warning signs

Do not normalize ringing or muffled sound.

Ringing or buzzing, muffled hearing after an event, difficulty following conversation, or trouble hearing high-pitched sounds are reasons to take exposure seriously. Temporary improvement does not make repeated episodes harmless. Noise-induced hearing loss is often gradual and permanent.

Reduce further loud exposure and seek assessment from a qualified hearing professional when ringing persists or hearing changes are noticeable. Sudden hearing loss—especially in one ear—needs urgent medical attention. This article supports prevention and awareness; it cannot diagnose an individual hearing problem.

A better listening ritual

Attention can replace intensity.

Choose a quiet place, lower the volume until it feels slightly restrained, and let the ear adapt for a few minutes. Listen for space, rhythm, texture, and words instead of using loudness as the main source of impact. Save high-energy listening for shorter periods and follow it with quiet.

Protecting hearing is not an argument against music. It is a way of keeping access to music, voices, birds, instruments, and the subtle sounds of the world for longer. The best listening technology helps that relationship endure.
