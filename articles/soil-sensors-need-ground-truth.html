A soil sensor is a question, not an answer

How growers can combine moisture sensors, field observation, weather, and simple records to irrigate with more confidence and less false precision.

Published 2026-09-06Updated 2026-09-06
  • Sustainable farming
  • Soil moisture
  • Field technology

The hidden variation

One field can contain several different water stories.

A moisture sensor measures a small volume of soil. Roots, stones, cracks, organic matter, shade, slope, compaction, and irrigation uniformity operate beyond that volume. Two reliable sensors can report different values because they are both accurately describing different places.

This is why a sensor reading should begin an investigation rather than end one. The useful question is not only, “What is the number?” It is, “Is this location representative, how is the trend changing, and does the crop and soil agree?”

Placement

Measure where decisions are actually made.

Choose monitoring locations that represent the main soil and irrigation conditions, then separately watch known problem areas. Place sensors within the active root zone and, when practical, at more than one depth. A shallow reading can show water arriving; a deeper reading can reveal whether it reached the roots or moved below them.

Do not place a sensor beside a leaking emitter, at the edge of a wetted pattern, in a wheel track, or in a low spot unless that condition is the intended subject. Record the depth, soil texture, crop stage, installation date, and distance from the water source. Without that context, a clean graph can preserve a bad assumption for an entire season.

Ground truth

Calibrate the instrument against the place.

At installation, after irrigation, and as the soil dries, compare the reading with a hand sample near the sensor. The USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service describes the feel-and-appearance method as one irrigation-scheduling tool and recommends soil-moisture monitoring through the season to support scheduling and calibration.

Watch trends before choosing thresholds. The same raw value can mean something different in sand, loam, clay, or an uncalibrated low-cost probe. Note rainfall, irrigation duration, crop condition, and any cultivation around the sensor. A useful record connects the signal with what happened in the field.

  • Check readings after installation and after unusually wet or dry events.
  • Compare multiple depths and representative locations.
  • Look for the expected rise after watering and decline during drying.
  • Investigate flat lines, sudden jumps, and values that never change.
  • Revisit thresholds when roots deepen or the crop stage changes.

Automation

Let alerts earn trust before they control water.

A sensible first system sends an alert and records a recommendation. The grower confirms it against the crop, weather forecast, water availability, and recent irrigation. Only after the system has been observed across changing conditions should it be allowed to control a valve, and even then it needs limits.

Use maximum run times, flow checks, manual override, and an alert for communication loss. Decide what happens when a battery dies or a sensor reports an impossible value. The safest default is usually to stop automatic action, preserve the last trustworthy state, and ask for human inspection rather than silently guessing.

  • Set a hard maximum irrigation duration.
  • Use flow data or a physical check to confirm that commanded water arrived.
  • Reject readings outside the sensor’s credible range.
  • Log manual overrides and the reason for them.
  • Keep a non-digital way to operate the system.

A one-season experiment

Improve one decision, then expand.

Select one irrigation zone. Install sensors at documented depths, keep a simple weekly field log, and record how each irrigation decision was made. At the end of the season, compare water applications, crop observations, failures, and labour with the previous practice. The purpose is not to prove that the technology was right. It is to learn when it was useful.

Good agricultural technology does not remove the farmer from the land. It makes patterns easier to see, scarce water easier to account for, and local knowledge easier to carry forward.
