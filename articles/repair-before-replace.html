The greenest upgrade may be the one you do not buy

A practical path for keeping electronics useful longer, protecting personal data, and sending truly finished devices into responsible reuse or recycling.

Published 2026-09-06Updated 2026-09-06
  • Repair
  • E-waste
  • Circular technology

The scale

The world is discarding electronics faster than it is learning to recover them.

The Global E-waste Monitor reports that the world generated 62 million tonnes of electronic waste in 2022. Only 22.3 percent was documented as formally collected and recycled. Under its business-as-usual projection, annual e-waste reaches 82 million tonnes by 2030.

An old phone is not only a small object in a drawer. It contains materials that required mining, processing, manufacturing, and transport, as well as personal data that may outlive the user’s attention. The most useful response begins before disposal: buy less often, maintain what already exists, and make the eventual handoff deliberate.

Before buying

Treat longevity as a feature you can investigate.

A low purchase price can hide an expensive short life. Before buying, look for a realistic software-support period, replaceable battery options, available parts and manuals, standard fasteners and ports, upgradeable storage or memory where relevant, and a manufacturer take-back route. Reviews that show disassembly and long-term support are often more valuable than launch-day benchmarks.

Also test the need. A battery replacement, storage cleanup, memory upgrade, lighter operating system, or repaired connector may solve the actual problem. The United States Environmental Protection Agency explicitly advises considering hardware or software upgrades before buying a new computer.

  • Write down the task the current device can no longer perform.
  • Check the repair cost before comparing replacement prices.
  • Prefer a supported refurbished device when it meets the need.
  • Avoid accessories and proprietary formats that lock the next purchase to this one.
  • Keep the box only if it meaningfully supports repair, resale, or safe transport.

A maintenance hour

Small care can recover years of useful life.

Once every few months, install security updates, remove unused software, verify backups, clean vents and ports without liquids or metal tools, inspect cables, and check the battery for heat, swelling, or physical damage. A swollen battery should not be pressed, punctured, charged, or mailed casually; stop using the device and follow local hazardous-battery guidance.

Keep a short record of the model, serial number, purchase date, repairs, and supported operating-system version. That record helps a repairer, increases resale confidence, and tells the next user what they are receiving.

The exit path

Reuse first; recycle what cannot safely remain in use.

If the device is functional and still supported, a known second user or reputable refurbishment programme can preserve more of its value than material recovery alone. Back up what matters, sign out of services, remove SIM and memory cards, disable activation locks, perform the manufacturer’s secure erase or factory-reset process, and verify that setup begins as a new device.

When reuse or repair is not viable, use a recognized local electronics collection or manufacturer take-back programme. Do not place lithium-ion batteries or devices containing them in household rubbish or ordinary recycling bins. Requirements differ by location, so the collector—not a generic internet list—should confirm what it accepts and how batteries must be handled.

A household device map

Make the forgotten drawer visible.

Circular technology is not a perfect loop, and recycling cannot erase the impact of making a product. But a device that stays useful longer asks less often for another mine, factory, shipment, and purchase. Longevity is one of the quietest forms of technical progress.

  • Collect unused devices without powering damaged or swollen batteries.
  • Mark each item: keep, repair, donate or sell, recycle, or data check needed.
  • Give every repair a cost limit and a decision date.
  • Erase and transfer reusable items while they still have value.
  • Record the recycler or take-back programme used for the remainder.
