Security Is a Form of Care

I approach security as a way to reduce avoidable harm, not a contest in technical capability. These notes focus on permissions, recovery, privacy and the everyday choices that make systems safer to use.

The principle

Security knowledge creates responsibility, not entitlement

I think about security as the work of reducing avoidable harm. Knowing how a system can be accessed does not mean access is permitted. The goal is not to demonstrate capability or collect as much information as possible. It is to protect people, services, and data while using the least access necessary.

Good security should preserve privacy, dignity, and the ability to recover. A control that frightens users into hiding mistakes can make an incident harder to contain. A clear, respectful process helps people report problems earlier and gives operators more time to respond.

Start with identity

Protect the accounts that control other accounts

Email, password managers, cloud identity, source-code hosting, and administrator accounts often sit at the centre of a person’s or team’s digital life. If these accounts are compromised, an attacker may reset other logins, access projects, or create new persistence.

  • Use a password manager and unique passwords where passwords remain necessary.
  • Use passkeys or phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication where available; prioritize the highest-impact accounts.
  • Secure recovery email, phone numbers, backup codes, and hardware keys.
  • Review active sessions, connected apps, and account recovery options periodically.
  • Keep devices and browsers updated, and encrypt devices that contain sensitive information.

Access control

Make powerful access rare, specific, and reviewable

Authentication answers “who is signing in?” Authorization answers “what may they do?” A trusted identity should not automatically receive unrestricted access. Least privilege means giving people, processes, and services only the permissions required for their current responsibilities.

  • Use separate administrator and everyday accounts when practical.
  • Prefer named identities over shared accounts; make ownership and accountability clear.
  • Grant temporary access an expiry date and remove it when the task ends.
  • Protect API keys, SSH private keys, tokens, and secrets; never commit them to a repository.
  • Review service accounts, integrations, OAuth scopes, and vendor access on a schedule.
  • Log meaningful administrative actions without placing passwords, tokens, or unnecessary personal data in logs.

Human factors

Urgency and familiarity are common attack tools

Phishing and social engineering often attempt to make a person act before checking a request. The message may imitate a colleague, a service provider, an employer, a financial institution, or a routine security prompt. Repeated sign-in prompts can also be used to pressure someone into approving access.

  1. Pause when a request is urgent, unusual, or asks for credentials, money, or privileged access.
  2. Verify through a separate trusted channel rather than replying to the suspicious message.
  3. Open the service directly from a bookmark or known address instead of following a login link.
  4. Report the event using the organization’s established process.
  5. If someone makes a mistake, focus first on containment and recovery; blame delays reporting.

Data minimization

The safest sensitive data may be data you never collect

Every retained record creates storage, access-control, backup, breach, and deletion obligations. Collect information for a clear purpose, restrict access, define retention, and remove it when there is no longer a legitimate reason to keep it.

This applies to project logs, network inventory, support tickets, AI prompts, analytics, and screenshots. Before sharing a log with an assistant or a public issue tracker, remove access tokens, internal addresses where sensitive, customer details, personal identifiers, and infrastructure secrets.

Operations

Build systems that assume ordinary mistakes will happen

A secure workflow should not rely on every person being alert all day. Use safe defaults, clear boundaries, repeatable changes, and observable results. Test changes in a lab where possible. Use backups, staged deployments, documented approvals, and rollback instructions for high-impact systems.

  • Keep services and dependencies updated through a known process.
  • Back up important data and test that it can actually be restored.
  • Separate development, test, and production access.
  • Validate input, fail safely, and avoid exposing internal diagnostic details to public users.
  • Review third-party libraries and integrations according to the risk they introduce.

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework is a useful structure for identifying, protecting, detecting, responding, and recovering. Adapt it to the scale of the system instead of copying a large-enterprise process without thought.

When something goes wrong

Contain, preserve evidence, recover, and learn

If an account or device may be compromised, use a trusted device for recovery where possible. Protect the main email or identity account, revoke suspicious sessions and tokens, change exposed credentials, review recovery methods, and notify the relevant service or security contact. For organizations, follow the incident plan and legal or contractual reporting obligations.

  1. Record what was observed and when; preserve relevant logs if it is safe to do so.
  2. Contain the affected account, device, or service to reduce further access.
  3. Remove unauthorized access paths and check for persistence, not just the first suspicious login.
  4. Restore from trusted backups or known-good configurations, then verify functionality.
  5. Review what failed and improve controls, communication, and recovery instructions.

Do not destroy evidence unnecessarily or run unfamiliar “cleanup” commands copied from a forum. When the incident affects critical services, sensitive data, or other people, escalate to qualified incident responders.

Professional boundaries

Capability, access, and authorization are different things

Security testing needs a defined scope, permission, time window, and agreed handling of findings. Do not scan or disrupt networks, inspect someone’s private information, test passwords, capture traffic, or change systems just because the tools make it possible. Work only on systems you own or are explicitly authorized to assess.

If you discover a vulnerability, minimize unnecessary access, document the issue safely, and use a responsible disclosure channel. Avoid publishing secrets or exploit details that create risk before maintainers have had a reasonable chance to respond.

Daily baseline

A short, repeatable security checklist

  • Update devices and critical applications.
  • Use unique credentials and strong sign-in protection.
  • Review unusual prompts before approving them.
  • Keep secrets out of source code, logs, and public screenshots.
  • Use a separate, authorized environment for risky testing.
  • Know where to report an incident and how to recover.
  • Review access when a role, device, project, or vendor relationship changes.

Security that works in everyday life is quieter than security theatre: clear controls, limited access, respectful support, and a recovery plan people can follow.
